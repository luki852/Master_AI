Here I collected possible exam questions that may be helpful for a meaningful preparation.
The exam will consist of 10–12 multiple-choice questions and last 70 minutes. You are
allowed to bring a two-page handwritten cheat sheet, and a calculator. Think of this list as a
map of the entire course, not a checklist to memorise.

Curse of dimensionality (cod) in high dimensions:

   1. Describe the standard setting of supervised machine learning. What are the true risk
        and the Bayes-optimal predictor? What is the role of the model class and the
        empirical risk? Intuitively, what are suitable notions of model complexity for neural
        networks and smooth function classes (e.g. Sobolev)? (No formulas needed.)
   2.   Illustrate graphically the classical and the modern bias–variance trade-off. How does
        model-class complexity interact with approximation error, estimation error, and
        generalisation? Where does the modern (double-descent) regime differ qualitatively
        from the classical picture? Can you give a plausible explanation for this (keyword:
        implicit regularization)? Would the interpolation threshold shift, if more training
        samples are used?
   3.   What is the geometric reason behind the cod? Why do local methods fail in high-
        dimensional learning? Illustrate, in pictures or words, how one can construct a family
        of Lipschitz functions whose estimation error grows rapidly with dimension.
   4.   Illustrate with pictures the error decompositions for smooth (i.e. Sobolev) and
        Lipschitz function classes. In which term does the cod appear in each case?
   5.   Sketch the loss landscape of a general high-dimensional optimisation problem. How
        does the picture change for the loss landscapes typically encountered when training
        deep neural networks?
   6.   State the manifold hypothesis in your own words. Why is it a potential remedy for the
        curse of dimensionality, and what does it assume about the geometry of real-world
        data?

        Geometric priors:

   7. How is a signal on a domain formally defined? What crucial mathematical structure
       does the space of signals carry?
   8. Give a formal definition of a group. Which axioms must the group operation satisfy?
       What concept allows us to speak about structural equivalence between two groups?
   9. Example: Give an example of a group homomorphism between matrices and
       scalars. Provide an isomorphism between an additive and a multiplicative group, and
       discuss that it satisfies the homomorphism property.
   10. What properties must a group action on a domain satisfy? How is the group action
       lifted from the domain to the level of signals (i.e., what is a group representation)?
       What are the crucial properties of this construction, and why is the existence of group
       inverses indispensable?
   11. Let X and Y be signal spaces with associated group representations. Define
       equivariance and invariance of a map f : X → Y precisely in formulas and via a
       commutative diagram. Illustrate the concept with a picture: how do signals from
       different orbits appear in feature space for an equivariant map?
   12. What operator maps a general hypothesis class (which transforms with the group) to
       a group-invariant one? How is this operator defined precisely for a finite group?
       When using group-invariant functions: how does the approximation error compare
       between the original and the invariant hypothesis class? What can happen to the
       estimation error? Which of the two errors may still suffer from the curse of
       dimensionality, and why? (Rough qualitative arguments suffice.)
   13. Explain the principle of scale separation (coarse graining) for signals. What are the
       two fundamental building blocks of multiscale priors? Why does combining symmetry
       with scale separation provide a more powerful prior than either principle alone?
   14. Describe the Geometric Deep Learning blueprint. What desiderata should an ideal
       hypothesis class of neural networks satisfy? Name the main building blocks of the
       blueprint, explain the role of each, and state the order in which they commonly
       appear in the compositional architecture.
   15. Sketch in a picture what it means for a map to be approximately invariant to
       transformations that are approximately in the group. How is this notion used to
       characterise the stability of learned representations?

Sets:

   16. What is the appropriate symmetry group acting on a set of elements? How do signals
       on sets look, and how can the group representation on the space of set signals be
       expressed?
   17. Example: Suppose a set signal is given as a feature matrix (one row per element). If
       the set elements are permuted by a permutation, how must the representing matrix
       transform? Write down the corresponding permutation matrix and explain how it acts
       on the feature matrix.
   18. How is linear equivariance characterised in terms of permutation matrices? What is
       the general form of a linear equivariant map on set signals?
   19. Describe the DeepSets architecture in terms of the GDL blueprint. Which
       components are equivariant and which are invariant? Which parts are
       fixed/learnable?

Graphs:

   20. Give a formal definition of an undirected node-attributed graph. How can it be fully
       represented by matrices? What ingredient does a graph add compared to a plain set
       of nodes?
   21. What is the appropriate symmetry group for graphs? How does it act on node and
       edge features in a transparent way? How can graph-level invariance and node-level
       equivariance be described in terms of this group representation?
   22. Explain the main ingredients of message passing in a graph neural network. What is
       the key difference compared to the set case, and what role do edges play?
   23. What is a graph convolution? Briefly name other flavours of graph neural networks.
       What are the main components of a message-passing neural network (MPNN) in
       general?
   24. Example: given a small labelled graph, compute the output of one message-passing
       layer for all nodes (you may choose a simple aggregation such as sum or mean).
       What happens to the result if the nodes are permuted?
   25. What is the relationship between MPNNs and the WL test? What property must an
       MPNN's aggregation function satisfy for the network to match the discriminative
       power of WL?
   26. Example: apply the Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) colour-refinement test to two given
       graphs for a fixed number of iterations. Interpret the outcome: what does the test tell
       you if the colour histograms differ? What can you conclude if they agree?
   27. What is the main idea of positional encoding approaches, and how can they
       overcome limitations of the WL test? Briefly explain the concept of graph
       substructure networks. (No formulas needed.)
   28. What is the main idea of subgraph GNNs? How do they connect to the graph
       reconstruction conjecture, and what does that conjecture say intuitively?
   29. In what sense can Transformers be viewed as an instance of MPNN? Is the attention
       mechanism, as applied in practice, truly permutation-equivariant?
   30. Explain the phenomenon of oversquashing, both intuitively and (briefly) in formulas.
       Which structural properties of an MPNN contribute to oversquashing, and which has
       the largest influence (no formulas required)?

Grids:

   31. What graph structure underlies a 1-D grid? What is the appropriate symmetry group,
       and how can signals on a grid be expressed? How does the associated group
       representation act on signals?
   32. Example: let I be a feature matrix representing an image (e.g., of a letter), and let I'
       be the same image shifted by one pixel. What must matrices A and B look like so that
       I' = AIB? Describe the structure of A and B.
   33. How do linear translation-equivariant maps on grids relate to the shift operator? How
       do they relate to cyclic (i.e. convolution) matrices? Explain the relation to the discrete
       Fourier transform. How can a discrete convolution be computed efficiently in the
       Fourier domain?
   34. For the continuous Fourier transform: how are localisation in space and localisation
       in frequency related? What does the Heisenberg uncertainty principle state in this
       context? Sketch the space–frequency diagram (spectrogram) of the standard, and
       windowed Fourier transform.
   35. Explain the instability of the Fourier modulus under small dilations. Sketch a typical
       spectrogram for a slightly dilated signal versus the original. How do low and high
       frequencies behave differently under dilation?
   36. Wavelets: explain in pictures how dilation and translation act on a mother wavelet
       (e.g. Haar), both in space and in frequency. Compare the spectrograms of the plain
       Fourier transform, the windowed Fourier transform, and the wavelet transform. Why
       are wavelets particularly well suited for detecting localised, edge-like structures?
   37. Wavelet scattering: what are the main building blocks? Why is the modulus
       nonlinearity crucial (what would be lost without it)? How does the spectrogram of the
       scattering transform differ from that of a plain wavelet transform, and what additional
       stability properties are gained?
Group convolutions:

   38. What is the guiding philosophy of convolution as a "transform-and-match" operation?
       Write the group convolution as an integral over the domain. What changes in higher
       layers when group convolution is applied twice in a row (i.e., what is the domain of
       the resulting feature maps)?
   39. Illustrate group convolution equivariance with a picture showing how a signal and a
       discretely rotated copy of it lead to correspondingly rotated output feature maps.
   40. Describe group convolution on the roto-translation group p4 (four discrete rotations).
       What is different in the first layer compared to higher layers? (No formulas needed.)
   41. What is a homogeneous space, and what does it mean for a group action to be
       transitive? What is the stabiliser subgroup of a point? Give two concrete examples of
       homogeneous spaces and identify their stabiliser subgroups.

Manifolds + spectral convolutions:

   42. From a practical perspective, why are manifolds a useful model? Explain informally
       what a smooth manifold is as a topological object: what is the role of a chart, and
       what is the tangent space at a point? What additional ingredient is needed to
       meaningfully work with tangent vectors?
   43. What is a Riemannian metric, and why is it useful? What does it mean for a quantity
       to be intrinsic on a Riemannian manifold? Define geodesics and the geodesic
       distance on a connected Riemannian manifold. Are these objects intrinsic?
   44. Define deformations and the notion of a metric isometry for Riemannian manifolds.
       Qualitatively discuss, how does the notion of stability under deformations compare
       between manifolds and homogeneous spaces.
   45. How is the gradient of a scalar field defined, what is its geometric interpretation, and
       why is the definition intrinsic? What information does the divergence of a vector field
       carry? How are the divergence and gradient operators related (think about
       adjointness)? What is the geometric meaning of the Laplace–Beltrami operator, and
       how can it be defined intrinsically? From an operator-theoretic perspective, why is the
       Laplacian particularly convenient to work with?
   46. How is spectral convolution defined, and why is it intrinsic? In what sense is naive
       spectral convolution unstable under domain deformations, and how can stable
       spectral filters be designed?
   47. What is a natural way to discretise a Riemannian manifold? What allows us to talk
       about intrinsic objects in this discrete setting? What is the general notion of a
       Laplacian on these discretised objects, and why is the mesh Laplacian a useful
       analog to the continuous Riemannian case (no formulas)?
   48. Interpret the graph or mesh Laplacian in terms of the GDL blueprint. In what sense is
       it an instance of message passing?
   49. Explain the main steps of the graph (or mesh) Fourier transform. What is a
       fundamental computational limitation of this approach? How can polynomial spectral
       filters overcome this limitation while retaining a convolutional flavour? What is the
       remaining overall limitation of spectral filters, even when they are designed to be
       stable under domain deformations?
